Hybrid Classical-Quantum Transfer Learning with Noisy Quantum Circuits

Summary in one paragraph

Hybrid classical-quantum transfer learning keeps a frozen pretrained convolutional network as the feature extractor and trains a small variational quantum circuit as the classifier head. This paper turns that recipe into a controlled benchmark: four frozen backbones (ResNet-18, MobileNet-V2, EfficientNet-B0, RegNet-400MF) are coupled to seven classification heads — three classical baselines and four quantum heads written in both PennyLane and Qiskit — and evaluated on four heterogeneous binary image datasets (Hymenoptera, Brain Tumor MRI, Cats vs. Dogs, Solar Dust) under three execution environments: ideal simulation, noise emulation calibrated on IBM Heron r2 specifications, and real IBM quantum hardware (ibm_torino). Over 16 dataset–backbone conditions and 5 random seeds, a Friedman test rejects the hypothesis that the seven heads perform equally (χ²(6) = 40.43, p = 3.75 × 10⁻⁷); the Nemenyi post-hoc analysis places the ideal PennyLane head in the same statistical group as the strongest classical heads. The authors therefore report quantum heads that are statistically comparable to classical transfer learning at matched parameter count — not superior — and quantify how much calibrated noise, phase damping in particular, and real hardware erode that parity.

How the benchmark is built

  • 4 frozen backbones: ResNet-18, MobileNet-V2, EfficientNet-B0, RegNet-400MF (ImageNet-pretrained, weights frozen, 224×224 inputs).
  • 7 classification heads: Linear, MLP-SM, MLP-LG (classical) · PL-Ideal, PL-Noisy, QK-Ideal, QK-Noisy (4-qubit, depth-3 variational circuits in PennyLane and Qiskit).
  • 4 datasets: Hymenoptera (ants vs. bees), Brain Tumor MRI, Cats vs. Dogs, Solar Dust — medical, biological, general-vision and industrial domains.
  • 3 execution environments: ideal statevector simulation · noisy emulation calibrated on IBM Heron r2 (single-qubit depolarizing p₁q = 0.02%, two-qubit p₂q = 0.5%, readout/SPAM error 1.2%, plus amplitude and phase damping) · real hardware on ibm_torino (133-qubit Heron r2, 4 qubits used, transpiled depth ≈ 49, 1024 shots, dynamical decoupling).
  • Statistics: 5 random seeds per configuration, Friedman test over the 16 dataset–backbone conditions, Nemenyi post-hoc comparisons at α = 0.05 (critical difference CD = 2.25).
  • Extras: per-noise-channel decomposition, scalability with qubit count and circuit depth, and a barren-plateau check.

Frequently asked questions

Does quantum transfer learning outperform classical transfer learning?
Not in this benchmark. Across 16 dataset–backbone conditions the Friedman test rejects equivalence of the seven heads (χ²(6) = 40.43, p = 3.75 × 10⁻⁷), but the Nemenyi post-hoc analysis (CD = 2.25) puts the ideal PennyLane quantum head in the same statistical group as the strongest classical heads (MLP-SM and MLP-LG). Ideal quantum heads reach accuracy comparable to classical baselines with a matched parameter count; the paper explicitly states that its statistics do not support a claim of consistent quantum advantage.
What is quantum transfer learning?
Quantum transfer learning is a hybrid classical-quantum training scheme in which a pretrained classical network — typically a CNN — is frozen and used as a fixed feature extractor, while a small parametrized quantum circuit (a variational quantum classifier) is trained on the resulting embeddings as the classification head. Because only the quantum head's parameters are trained, the approach fits NISQ-era hardware: in this paper each head uses just four qubits and depth three.
How much does noise degrade the quantum heads?
Substantially, and unevenly. Under emulation calibrated on IBM Heron r2 specifications, quantum heads lose accuracy relative to their ideal counterparts — for example PennyLane on RegNet-400MF/Hymenoptera drops from 97.7% ideal to 82.1% noisy, and ResNet-18 from 96.4% to 90.3% — while other combinations are nearly unaffected (EfficientNet-B0: 96.9% → 96.2%). The per-channel decomposition identifies phase damping as the channel with the largest aggregate impact, followed by depolarizing and amplitude damping. The degradation is much larger than the loss of the classical baselines.
How does it behave on real IBM quantum hardware?
The real-hardware campaign ran on ibm_torino, a 133-qubit IBM Heron r2 processor, using 4 qubits with transpiled circuit depth of about 49, 1024 shots per circuit and dynamical decoupling as error mitigation. Results are reported for Hymenoptera. The Qiskit pipeline is more resilient than the PennyLane pipeline on every backbone, with a consistent gap of 5.8 to 6.9 percentage points (MobileNet-V2: 95.32% vs 88.45%; ResNet-18: 92.15% vs 86.32%; RegNet-400MF: 87.43% vs 81.24%; EfficientNet-B0: 74.62% vs 68.42%). Real-hardware training averages 575 s per run for Qiskit and 686 s for PennyLane, i.e. slower than simulation but still feasible for moderate dataset sizes.
Which is faster, PennyLane or Qiskit?
It depends on the execution mode, and the paper reports the crossover explicitly. In ideal simulation PennyLane is faster: on Brain Tumor with ResNet-18, 869.5 ± 51.7 s versus 1098.8 ± 3.7 s for Qiskit, both using the SPSA gradient estimator, so the difference comes from the simulator backend rather than the optimizer. In noisy simulation the ordering flips because Qiskit's AerEstimatorV2 runs on the C++ Aer backend: on Hymenoptera with ResNet-18, QK-noisy takes 188.3 ± 14.9 s versus 214.9 ± 1.5 s for QK-ideal, even though the noisy run also samples 1024 shots per evaluation. On real hardware Qiskit is again faster (575 s vs 686 s per run on average). Classical heads, for reference, finish training in under 730 s.
Do the circuits suffer from barren plateaus?
The circuits used in the main experiments are 4-qubit, depth-3 variational heads, which sit comfortably below the qubit counts and depths at which barren plateaus typically manifest, so vanishing gradients do not explain the results. The paper nevertheless treats scalability as a separate axis, studying accuracy and gradient behaviour as qubit count and circuit depth grow, and discusses barren plateaus as the main obstacle to extending the approach to wider registers.
Is the paper open access, and can the results be reproduced?
Yes. The article is published open access under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International licence (CC BY 4.0) by Tech Science Press, and a preprint is available on arXiv (2603.16973). Both a mirrored PDF and the publisher PDF are freely downloadable; the paper documents the full grid — 4 backbones × 7 heads × 4 datasets × 5 seeds × 3 execution environments — together with the unified hyperparameters (Adam, η = 10⁻³, batch size 16, StepLR), the Heron r2 calibration values and the exact hardware execution configuration.
Which datasets and backbones are used?
Four binary image datasets — Hymenoptera (ants vs. bees), Brain Tumor MRI, Cats vs. Dogs, and Solar Dust — chosen to span medical, biological, general-vision and industrial domains with different sample sizes and difficulty. Four ImageNet-pretrained backbones are frozen as feature extractors: ResNet-18, MobileNet-V2, EfficientNet-B0 and RegNet-400MF.
